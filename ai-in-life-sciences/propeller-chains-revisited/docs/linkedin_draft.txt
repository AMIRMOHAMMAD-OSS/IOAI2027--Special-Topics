What should a generative model know about a molecule before it proposes how that molecule moves?

I’m exploring this question in an AI in Life Sciences course I’m designing for the National Olympiad on Artificial Intelligence committee, for students preparing for IOAI 2027.

For one small case study, I returned to my supervisor Dr. Ariel B. Lindner’s first paper: his 1993 work with Flavio Grynszpan and Silvio E. Biali on rotations in molecular propeller chains.

The teaching problem is concrete: how can a four-ring chain change handedness while respecting molecular geometry, atom identity and energy?

I first built a classical MMFF94 reference. A complete localized candidate reaches 7.404 kcal/mol above the starting minimum; two synchronized candidates reach 15.566 and 15.514 kcal/mol on the same energy reference. The complete-path checks now also pass on my Mac Studio.

The most useful lesson appeared between the optimized structures. A sequence of converged configurations can still jump between different conformational branches. A smooth energy plot alone can hide a missing molecular connection.

We therefore need to test the connecting motion itself. Here, that meant following the branches, verifying four constrained saddle connections, preserving atom identities, and checking energies between saved structures.

For the course, this gives us a concrete question to ask of a future generative model: can its proposed motion satisfy the same geometric and energetic checks?

These results are classical force-field calculations; the generative-model comparison is future work. The original paper used MM2, and this MMFF94 study compares selected candidates without establishing global optimality or experimental rates.

Code, data, animations and validation records are assembled as Propeller Chains Revisited, with AI-assisted coding documented.
